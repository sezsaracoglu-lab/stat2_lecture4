Looking at Data: Relationships

Chapter 2 — scatterplots, correlation, least-squares regression, and every way the two can mislead you.

A note before you start, Sezen

Everything you did before this lecture was about one variable at a time: one mean, one spread, one distribution. From here on we look at pairs of variables measured on the same people — and the whole of psychology lives in those pairs. Does time outdoors go with better motor skills? Does an assessment-centre score go with performance two years later? The machinery is small: one picture (the scatterplot) and two numbers (r and the regression line). The difficulty is never the arithmetic. It is knowing what those numbers quietly refuse to tell you.

That matters directly in your work at NN. When a selection test correlates .30 with job performance, someone in the room will say "so the test predicts performance". Lesson 13 gives you the honest version of that sentence (it explains 9% of the variation), and Lesson 14 gives you the three pictures that produce the same association — causation, common response and confounding — and only the first is a clean causal arrow from x to y. A lurking variable in a talent dataset is not a statistical curiosity — it is usually the whole story.

🧵 Synthesis

The single thesis of this lecture

A relationship between two quantitative variables is described by one picture and two numbers — and every warning in this chapter exists because the numbers cannot see the picture. The correlation r measures straight-line closeness only, so it under-reports a perfect curve — and reports near zero for a symmetric U — while reporting an impressive number for a cloud with a single stray point dragging it. The least-squares line will fit itself to any data you hand it, will swing towards whichever point sits furthest out in x, and will cheerfully predict a thirty-year-old woman at 6'10" if you ask it about an age it has never seen. r² tells you how much of y the line accounts for; the residual plot tells you whether a line was ever the right shape; and none of it tells you that x caused y, because causation, common response and confounding all produce exactly the same scatterplot. So the discipline of this lecture is a sequence, not a formula: plot first, describe in words, then compute — and only then, very carefully, explain.

Lesson 1 of 14 · 2.1 Relationships

🔗 Two variables at a time

What "associated" actually means, and the one question you must answer before you draw anything.

Slides 3, 5 and 7. The split on the right-hand side is the whole of Lecture 4: correlation is symmetric, regression is not.

  • 🔗
    The definition, word for word. Two variables measured on the same cases are associated if knowing the value of one tells you something about the other that you would not otherwise know.
  • 👥
    "Same cases" is doing real work. Two variables from two different groups of people cannot be associated — there is no pairing to look at.
  • 🎯
    A response variable measures an outcome of a study.
  • 🧭
    An explanatory variable explains or causes changes in the response variable.
  • ⚖️
    Sometimes there are no roles. If you only want to know whether two things go together, neither is explanatory — and correlation is the right tool.
🪝 Memory hook: association is a rumour — hearing one variable's name makes you expect something about the other. Explanatory and response is a job title: someone is doing the explaining and someone is being explained.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Two things are associated if knowing one changes what I'd guess about the other. Sometimes I just want to know they go together — that's correlation. Sometimes I want to use one to predict the other, and then I have to decide which one does the predicting — that's regression."
★ Key insight

Decide before you plot whether you are exploring or explaining, because that decision fixes which variable goes on which axis — and a scatterplot with the axes the wrong way round quietly answers a different question.

🎬 Explanatory vs response variables 🎬 Association between two variables

🧩 Comprehension check

A researcher records each student's screen time and their sleep quality. Is there an explanatory variable?
Only if the researcher's purpose is to explain sleep with screen time. If the question is simply "do these two go together?", both variables are on equal footing and correlation is appropriate. The data alone never tells you which is which — the research question does.
Why is "knowing one tells you something you would not otherwise know" a better definition than "they move together"?
Because it covers relationships that are not straight lines. A U-shaped relationship (stress and performance, say) does not "move together" in one direction, but knowing stress still tells you plenty about performance. Correlation would miss it; the definition does not.
What extra thing does the word "response" imply that "y variable" does not?
That it is an outcome — the thing being measured at the end, which the study is trying to account for. "y variable" is only a position on a graph; "response" is a claim about the structure of the study.

Lesson 2 of 14 · The flowchart

🗺️ Where correlation and regression live

The lecture opens and closes with the same decision tree. You should be able to find your test on it in five seconds.

Slides 4 and 6, redrawn as a grid. The original tree first asks for the data type, then for the purpose of the test — this grid shows the part of the flowchart this lecture lives in, with both questions visible at once — the related-samples and 3-or-more-group rows (Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis, Friedman, the paired t test and the ANOVAs) are left out.

  • 1️⃣
    First question: what kind of data? Nominal (categories you count), ordinal (ranks), or scale (interval/ratio, real numbers).
  • 2️⃣
    Second question: what are you trying to do? Describe one sample, look at a relationship, or compare groups.
  • 3️⃣
    Correlation and regression sit in one cell only: a relationship between scale variables, with one predictor.
  • 🚦
    The scale column has a gate. If the distributional assumptions fail, the flowchart sends you left to the ordinal alternatives — Spearman's r instead of Pearson's.
  • 🧮
    Same question, different data type, different test. "Are these two related?" is answered by Phi, Spearman or Pearson depending only on the measurement level.
🪝 Memory hook: the flowchart is a seating plan. The data type chooses your table; the purpose chooses your chair. Pearson's r has exactly one seat: scale data, one relationship, assumptions met.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Before picking a test I ask two things: what kind of numbers do I have, and what am I trying to do with them? Correlation and regression are only for proper numeric variables where I'm asking whether two things are related."
★ Key insight

Pearson's r is not "the correlation test" — it is the correlation test for scale data whose assumptions hold. Ranks get Spearman; two yes/no variables get Phi. Choosing r for the wrong measurement level is a content error, not a rounding error.

🎬 Choosing the right statistical test 🎬 Levels of measurement

🧩 Comprehension check

You have two variables: "preferred study location" (library / home / café) and "exam grade (A–F)". Where are you on the grid?
Nominal by ordinal — and the relationship row for nominal data offers only the Phi coefficient, which is for two binary variables. In practice this pair is handled as a two-way table with a χ² test of independence, which is the nominal cell in the "compare groups" row. Neither Pearson's r nor a regression line belongs anywhere near it.
Why does the flowchart ask about distributional assumptions only in the scale column?
Because only the scale tests (t, correlation, regression, ANOVA) make assumptions about the shape of the distribution. Rank-based and frequency-based tests do not, which is exactly why they are the fallback when the assumptions fail.

Lesson 3 of 14 · 2.2 Scatterplots

📈 Building the picture

The most useful graph in this chapter, and the three rules for drawing one.

The eight hikers from slide 11, plotted from the numbers in the table below. Body weight is explanatory, so it goes on the x axis; pack weight is the response, so it goes on y.

  • 🎯
    One dot per individual. A scatterplot shows two quantitative variables measured on the same individuals; each case is one point.
  • ↔️
    Step 1 — decide the axes. If a distinction exists, the explanatory variable goes on x and the response on y.
  • 🏷️
    Step 2 — label and scale. Both axes need a name, units and a sensible range.
  • ✏️
    Step 3 — plot the values. Nothing else: no line, no summary, no averaging.
  • 🔢
    Both variables must be quantitative. A scatterplot of "gender against pack weight" is not a scatterplot.

The scatterplot is not an illustration of the analysis — it is the first step of the analysis. Every caution later in this lecture (curves, outliers, influential points, extrapolation) is visible in the plot and invisible in the numbers.

🎬 Making and reading a scatterplot

🧩 Comprehension check

A classmate plots pack weight on x and body weight on y. Has she made an error?
She has broken the convention, not the mathematics. The correlation would be identical, because r makes no distinction between explanatory and response variables. But if she then fits a regression line she is predicting body weight from pack weight, which is a different model with a different slope — and almost certainly not the question she meant to ask.
Why does the plot need eight dots and not two boxplots?
Because boxplots would summarise each variable separately and throw away the pairing. The whole point is which pack weight went with which body weight — and that information only survives if each hiker stays a single point.

Lesson 4 of 14 · Interpreting scatterplots

🔍 Direction, form, strength — and the exception

Four words describe any scatterplot. Learn to say them in this order and you can answer the exam question before you compute anything.

Panels (b) and (d) are both round clouds: different data, same verdict. The contrast that matters is (a) and (c) — where the cloud has a tilt — against (b) and (d), where it has none.

  • ↗️
    Direction. Positively associated: above-average values of one go with above-average values of the other (and below with below). Negatively associated: above-average goes with below-average.
  • 📐
    Form. Linear, curved, clustered. This is the shape of the cloud, not its tilt.
  • 💪
    Strength. How closely the points follow that form. Tight to a line = strong; widely scattered = weak.
  • 🚨
    Departures. An outlier is an individual value that falls outside the overall pattern of the relationship.
  • 🗣️
    Say all four, in context. "There is a moderately strong, positive, linear relationship between body weight and pack weight, with one possible outlier."

Slide 18: the same hikers with the pattern drawn in. The 187-pound hiker carries relatively less than the pattern predicts — that is what "outside the overall pattern of the relationship" means. Computed from the slide's own table: r = 0.795 with that hiker, r = 0.946 without.

"Positively associated" is defined through above-average and below-average values, not through "goes up". That wording is what later turns into the product of two z-scores inside the formula for r — the definition and the formula are the same idea.

🎬 Direction, form, strength, outliers 🎬 Positive and negative association

🧩 Comprehension check

Is the 187-pound hiker an outlier in x, in y, or in the relationship?
In the relationship. His body weight is the largest in the group and his pack weight is unremarkable — neither value is impossible on its own. What is unusual is the combination: everyone else's pack rises with their body weight and his does not. That is why the slide says he "falls outside the overall pattern".
Can a relationship be strong and curved at the same time?
Yes, and this is the trap the next lesson sets. Strength is how closely the points follow the form. Points lying exactly on a parabola are a perfectly strong curved relationship — and Pearson's r will still report something unimpressive, because r only measures closeness to a straight line.
Why does the definition of positive association mention both above-average and below-average values?
Because a relationship has to hold across the whole range to count as a direction. If only the high scorers moved together and the low scorers were random, the cloud would not tilt as a whole — and the correlation would be much weaker than the top half alone suggests.

Lesson 5 of 14 · Nonlinear form · categorical variables

🌙 When the cloud bends, and when it splits

Two departures from the simple picture: a form that is not a line, and a third variable hiding inside the plot.

Both panels are illustrative redrawings of the shapes on slides 15 and 19 — the lecture gives the pictures, not the underlying numbers.

  • 🌙
    Not every form is a line. Slide 15's cloud curves upward — the relationship is obvious, and a straight line is simply the wrong description of it.
  • ⚠️
    Curvature is a plot-only finding. No summary number announces it. If you never draw the picture, you will never know.
  • 🎨
    Adding a categorical variable: use a different plot colour or symbol for each category — the slide's Southern states are orange dots.
  • 🧩
    Two clouds can masquerade as one. The overall tilt of a mixed plot may belong to the grouping, not to the relationship inside either group.
  • 🔎
    This is the visual version of a lurking variable — the third variable is right there in the plot, once you colour it in.

📊 The SAT example on slide 19

The slide plots each US state's mean SAT verbal score against the percent of high-school graduates taking the SAT, with Southern states drawn as orange dots. The overall cloud slopes downward: states where almost everybody sits the exam have lower mean scores. That is not evidence that taking the SAT harms performance — it is selection. Where few students sit it, those few are the ones aiming at selective universities.

Colouring the Southern states is the lecture's demonstration that a categorical variable can be carried inside a scatterplot without a third axis.

🪝 Memory hook: a curve is a road with a bend — driving straight through it is not a shortcut, it is a crash. A hidden category is a double exposure: two photographs printed on top of each other, and the pattern you "see" belongs to neither.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Sometimes the dots make a curve instead of a line, and then the correlation number is the wrong tool entirely. And sometimes the dots are really two separate groups — colour them in and the story changes completely."
★ Key insight

Every technique in the rest of this lecture assumes the form is linear. Checking the form is therefore not an optional refinement — it is the licence to use correlation and regression at all.

🎬 Nonlinear relationships 🎬 Adding a categorical variable

🧩 Comprehension check

Would Pearson's r be small for slide 15's curve?
Smaller than the relationship deserves, but not necessarily near zero. That curve rises throughout, so a straight line still captures a lot of it and r would come out high. The honest statement is that r understates a rising curve and can be near zero for a U-shape, where the rising half and the falling half cancel out. Either way, r is answering a question about straightness, not about strength of association.
The orange dots in the SAT plot sit mostly at the high-percentage, low-score end. What would you check next?
Whether the downward slope survives within each group. If both colours slope down on their own, the relationship is real in each; if each colour is flat and only the two groups differ, the apparent relationship is entirely between-group — a lurking variable wearing a disguise.

Lesson 6 of 14 · 2.3 Correlation

🧲 What the correlation r actually measures

Why the formula is a product of two z-scores, and what that product is doing in each corner of the plot.

Slide 21's formula, drawn. Each point contributes (xᵢ − x̄)/sx × (yᵢ − ȳ)/sy; r is their average, using n − 1.

  • 👁️
    Why we need a number at all. The slide is blunt: our eyes are not always good judges of how strong a relationship is — so r supplements the plot, it does not replace it.
  • 🧲
    r measures the strength of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables. Strength means closeness to a straight line.
  • 📏
    Standardising is what removes the units. Dividing by sx and sy turns pounds and points into "how many SDs from the mean", so r has no units.
  • 🤝
    Matching corners are positive. A person above average on both, or below average on both, contributes a positive product.
  • ⚔️
    Mismatched corners are negative. Above on one and below on the other subtracts. r is the net result across everybody.

📊 The same quantity, written two ways — and the pieces it is built from

FormFormulaWhen the lecture uses it
z-score form (slide 21)r = 1⁄n−1 Σ (xᵢ − x̄)/sx × (yᵢ − ȳ)/syTo explain why r works
SP / SS form (slide 27)r = SP ⁄ √(SSX · SSY)To compute r by hand
Sum of productsSP = Σ(X − MX)(Y − MY)The covariability of X and Y

They are algebraically identical. The first shows the logic; the second is what you would actually calculate in an exam.

🪝 Memory hook: r is an agreement score. Each person votes "we agree" (both above average, or both below) or "we disagree" (one of each), and the size of their vote is how extreme they are. r is the average vote.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "For every person I check whether they're above or below average on each variable. If people who are high on one tend to be high on the other, the products are positive and r is positive. r is basically the average of those agreements, scaled so it can never leave −1 to +1."
★ Key insight

Because r is built from deviations from the two means, it is blind to everything except the tilt of the cloud around its centre. That single fact generates every property and every caution in the next lesson.

🎬 Correlation through z-scores 🎬 Pearson's r explained

🧩 Comprehension check

A person sits exactly at the mean of x. How much do they contribute to r?
Nothing to the sum of products — their z-score for x is zero, so their product is zero however extreme their y value is. (They are not quite invisible: they still count in n and swell sy, which drags |r| down a little.) Points near the centre of the cloud barely affect r; points far out in both directions dominate it.
Why divide by n − 1 rather than n?
So that it cancels. The z-scores already divide by sx and sy, which themselves use n − 1, and the 1/(n − 1) in front is what cancels with them — leaving r = SP ⁄ √(SSX·SSY), the form that is capped at ±1. Use n in both places and you get exactly the same r; mix the two and the result can no longer reach 1 for a perfect line.
If you converted body weight from pounds to kilograms, what happens to r?
Nothing. Every x deviation and sx shrink by the same factor, so each z-score is unchanged. This is the formal reason r has no units — and it is property 2 in the next lesson.

Lesson 7 of 14 · Properties of r · cautions

📏 The number line from −1 to +1

Six properties, four cautions, and a calibration exercise for your eye.

The six values on slide 24, redrawn. Each panel is generated so its correlation is exactly the number printed underneath — the shapes are illustrative, the r values are not.

  • 📏
    r is always between −1 and +1, with r > 0 a positive association and r < 0 a negative one.
  • 🎯
    Near 0 means a very weak linear relationship; strength increases as r moves away from 0 towards either end.
  • 💯
    r = ±1 happens only for a perfect straight line — every point exactly on it.
  • 🔄
    r makes no distinction between explanatory and response. Swap the axes and r is unchanged.
  • 🚫
    r has no units and does not change if you change the units of x, y or both.

📊 The four cautions on slide 25 — and what each one costs you

CautionWhat goes wrong if you ignore it
Both variables must be quantitativeAn r computed on category codes (1 = library, 2 = home, 3 = café) is arithmetic on labels. Renumber the categories and r changes.

🪝 Memory hook: r is a thermometer for straightness. It reads high only if the points line up — it cannot tell you the room is on fire in some other shape.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "r runs from −1 to +1. The sign tells me the direction; how far it is from zero tells me how tightly the dots hug a straight line. It has no units, it doesn't care which variable I call x, and it can be wrecked by one weird case."
★ Key insight

"Weak r" and "weak relationship" are not the same claim. r near zero means no straight-line trend — which is either a weak relationship or a strong one of the wrong shape, and only the scatterplot can tell you which.

🎬 Properties of r 🎬 Anscombe's quartet — same r, different data

🧩 Comprehension check

Which is stronger: r = −0.7 or r = +0.5?
r = −0.7. Strength is distance from zero; the sign only gives the direction. This is the single most common slip in exam questions about "the strongest relationship".
Your colleague reports r = 1.4 for two questionnaire scales. What has happened?
An arithmetic error, without any doubt — r cannot leave the interval from −1 to +1. The usual culprits are dividing by SS instead of √(SSX·SSY), or mixing up SP and SS somewhere in the table.
Does r = 0.9 mean the relationship is nine times stronger than r = 0.1?
No. r is not a proportion of anything — that job belongs to r², which is 81% against 1%. Differences in r are not comparable as ratios, which is exactly why the lecture introduces the coefficient of determination later.

🧪 Demo 4 · The table, live

Change any number and watch the deviations, SP, SS and r recompute. Three presets are waiting: the lecturer's jury data, a perfect straight line, and a deliberately useless one.

  • 1️⃣
    Means first. MX = 13, MY = 6. Every other column is measured from these.
  • 2️⃣
    Deviations next. Each deviation column must sum to zero — a free check on your arithmetic.
  • 3️⃣
    SP is the product column. Σ(X − MX)(Y − MY) = 40. This is the only column where the two variables meet.
  • 4️⃣
    SSX and SSY are the squared columns, 44 and 40 — each variable's own variability.
  • 5️⃣
    Divide. r = 40 ⁄ √(44 × 40) = 0.953. The two judges rank the five cases almost identically — though note this is not "agreement": jury 2 scores everyone far lower than jury 1, and r is blind to that difference in level.

Exam-proof checks

  • Both deviation columns must add to 0.
  • SS columns can never be negative — they are sums of squares.
  • SP can be negative; that is what makes r negative.
  • |SP| can never exceed √(SSX·SSY). If it does, you have an arithmetic error — that inequality is why |r| ≤ 1.
  • Round only at the end: rounding the deviations first will move your r in the third decimal.
🪝 Memory hook: SP is the couples' dance, SS is each partner practising alone. r asks how much of the solo energy actually showed up in the dance.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I subtract each mean, multiply the two deviations for each person and add them up — that's SP. Then I square each deviation separately and add those up — those are the SS. r is SP divided by the square root of the two SS multiplied together."
★ Key insight

The denominator √(SSX·SSY) is the largest value SP could possibly take. That is the whole reason r is capped at 1: r is the fraction of the maximum possible covariability that actually occurred.

🎬 Calculating r by hand

🧩 Comprehension check

In the demo, set the five Y values to 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and the X values to 18, 16, 14, 12, 10. What is r, and why?
Exactly −1. Every X falls by 2 as every Y rises by 2, so the points lie perfectly on a descending straight line. SP is negative and equals −√(SSX·SSY) in size, which is the extreme case the properties list mentions.
Why does d3 contribute nothing to SP?
Because its Y value is exactly the mean, so (Y − MY) = 0 and the product is 0 regardless of X. A case at the centre of one variable has no vote on the correlation.
If jury 2 had scored everybody two points higher, what would change?
Nothing in r. Adding a constant shifts MY by the same amount, so every deviation — and therefore SP, SSY and r — is unchanged. r sees only deviations from the mean, never the level.

Lesson 9 of 14 · 2.4 Least-squares regression

📐 The line: b₀, b₁ and a prediction

Correlation treats both variables equally. Regression does not — and that asymmetry buys you the ability to predict.

Slides 31 and 32. A slope of 5 means that if x goes up by 1, y goes up by 5 — and a negative slope means it goes down.

  • ⚖️
    Correlation treats both variables equally. Regression has an independent (predictor) variable X and a dependent (outcome) variable Y.
  • 📏
    A regression line describes how y changes as x changes, and lets you predict y for a given x.
  • 🎩
    ŷ is a prediction, not an observation. "y-hat" is the predicted value of the response for a given x.
  • 📈
    b₁ is the slope — the amount by which y changes for each one-unit increase in x.
  • 📍
    b₀ is the intercept — the value of y when x = 0, which is often outside the data and meaningless on its own.

Slide 33: the sparrowhawk colonies. The fitted line printed on slide 47 is ŷ = 31.9343 − 0.3040x, where x is the percent of adults returning and ŷ the predicted number of new adult birds joining. The dots are illustrative — the slide shows the plot but not the individual colony values.

🧪 Demo 5 · Predict a colony

Move the slider to a percentage of returning adults and the line does its arithmetic out loud. Watch what the warning does when you leave the observed range.

percent of adults returning (x)60
🪝 Memory hook: b₀ is the starting salary and b₁ is the annual raise. The line tells you what you earn after x years — and nobody should ask about year −5.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "The regression line is a recipe: start at b₀ and add b₁ for every unit of x. Put a value of x in, get a predicted y out. Unlike correlation, it matters enormously which variable I chose to predict."
★ Key insight

Slide 47's answer — 31.9343 − (0.3040)(60) = 13.69 birds — is a prediction of the average number of new birds for colonies with 60% returning, not a promise about any single colony. Every regression prediction is a statement about a group mean.

🎬 Interpreting slope and intercept 🎬 Predicting with a regression line

🧩 Comprehension check

Interpret the slope −0.3040 in the colonies example in one sentence.
For every extra percentage point of adults returning, the predicted number of new adult birds joining the colony falls by about 0.30 birds. (Ten points more returning → about three fewer newcomers.)
The intercept is 31.93. Is that a meaningful number?
Only as arithmetic. It is the predicted number of new birds for a colony where no adults return — a situation outside the observed data, and arguably not a colony at all. Intercepts are frequently meaningless in isolation; they exist to position the line.
Why can't you use this same line to predict the percent returning from the number of new birds?
Because least squares minimises the vertical gaps in the direction of whichever variable you called y. Turning the question round produces a different line with a different slope. The distinction between explanatory and response is essential — that is Fact 3 in Lesson 11.

Lesson 10 of 14 · The least-squares criterion

🟥 Why these squares, and where b₁ comes from

"Least squares" names exactly what is being minimised. Once you can say the phrase in full, the formulas follow.

Slide 35, made visual. Because we are trying to predict y, we want the line as close as possible to the points in the vertical direction.

  • 🟥
    Squares, not distances. Squaring makes every gap positive and punishes big misses far more than small ones.
  • ⬇️
    Vertical, not perpendicular — because the quantity we are wrong about is y.
  • 🥇
    "Least" is literal. No other straight line gives a smaller total for this dataset.
  • 🧮
    b₁ = SP ⁄ SSX = r · (sY ⁄ sX) — the slope is the correlation, rescaled into the units of the two variables.
  • 🎯
    b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄, which forces the line through the point (x̄, ȳ).

🪝 Memory hook: the least-squares line is the laziest possible compromise: it cannot please everyone, so it settles where the total squared complaint is smallest.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "For any line I could draw, measure how far each point sits above or below it, square those gaps and add them up. The least-squares line is the one where that total is smallest — and there's a formula, so I never have to guess."
★ Key insight

b₁ = r · (sY/sX) says the slope and the correlation are the same fact in different clothes: r is the relationship in standard-deviation units, b₁ is that same relationship converted into the real units of your two variables.

🎬 Least squares, visually 🎬 Where b₁ = r·sy/sx comes from

🧩 Comprehension check

If r = 0.6, sX = 2 and sY = 10, what is the slope?
b₁ = 0.6 × (10/2) = 3. One extra unit of x predicts three more units of y — and notice that the same r would give a slope of 0.12 if the spreads were reversed. The slope depends on the units; r does not.
Why does squaring matter — why not minimise the sum of the absolute gaps?
You could (it is a real method), but squaring has two consequences the course relies on: it gives a single closed-form answer through b₁ = SP/SSX, and it makes large errors dominate, which is also why one far-out point can pull the line so hard in Lesson 12.
Both means are 0 in z-scores, so b₀ = 0. What does that tell you about a regression on standardised data?
That predicting is one multiplication: ẑy = r · zx. A person one SD above the mean on x is predicted to be r SDs above the mean on y — which is exactly Fact 1 in the next lesson.

Lesson 11 of 14 · Facts and assumptions

🪜 Three facts, four assumptions

Slides 38 and 39 in full. These are the most quotable lines in the lecture — expect at least one of them on the exam.

Slide 39, Fact 1: a change of one standard deviation in x corresponds to a change of r standard deviations in y.

  • 1️⃣
    Fact 1. A change of one SD in x corresponds to a change of r SDs in y. With r = 0.5, being a whole SD above average on x predicts only half an SD above average on y.
  • 2️⃣
    Fact 2. The least-squares line always passes through (x̄, ȳ) — the point of the two means.
  • 3️⃣
    Fact 3. The distinction between explanatory and response variables is essential. Regressing y on x and x on y give different lines.
  • 🧱
    The four assumptions: a linear relation; y normally distributed at each value of x; the variance of y the same at each value of x; and independent observations.
  • 🔁
    Least squares is everywhere — the slide calls regression one of the most common statistical settings, and least squares the most common method for fitting the line.

📊 The four assumptions, and how each one fails

AssumptionWhat it meansHow you would notice
Linear relationThe means of y really do fall on a straight line as x changes.A curve in the scatterplot, or a curve in the residual plot.
Normality at each xFor any fixed x, the y values scatter normally around the line.Strong skew or a long tail on one side of the line.
Equal varianceThe spread of y around the line is the same everywhere.A fan or funnel shape in the residual plot.
Independent observationsOne case's value tells you nothing about another's.Repeated measures on the same person, pupils inside the same class, days in a time series.

Note what is not on the list: nothing requires x to be normally distributed, and nothing requires y overall to be normal — only y at each value of x.

🪝 Memory hook: Fact 1 is a tax on prediction. You arrive one SD above average, the line takes its cut, and you are credited with only r SDs. The weaker the relationship, the higher the tax.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "If someone is one standard deviation above average on the predictor, I don't predict they'll be one SD above on the outcome — only r of an SD. Predictions always fall back towards the mean, and that's where the word regression comes from."
★ Key insight

Facts 1 and 3 are the same warning from two sides: regression is not a symmetric description of a relationship but a rule for predicting one named variable, and that rule is deliberately conservative. Swap the roles and you get a different, equally conservative rule.

🎬 Regression to the mean 🎬 The assumptions of linear regression

🧩 Comprehension check

A selection test correlates r = 0.4 with job performance. A candidate scores 2 SDs above the mean. What do you predict?
0.4 × 2 = 0.8 SDs above the mean performance — not 2. This is the single most useful sentence in this lecture for talent work: strong selection scores predict good but distinctly less extreme performance, and the gap is not a failure of the test, it is arithmetic.
Why must the line pass through (x̄, ȳ)?
Because b₀ is defined as ȳ − b₁x̄. Substitute x = x̄ into ŷ = b₀ + b₁x and you get exactly ȳ. It is not a coincidence but a rearrangement — and it gives you a free check on any line you fit by hand.
Which assumption is violated if you measure the same 20 children at five time points and treat all 100 rows as independent?
Independence. Each child contributes five correlated rows, so the effective sample size is nowhere near 100. The line itself may be fine; the standard errors and p-values will be far too optimistic.

Lesson 12 of 14 · 2.5 Cautions · outliers

🧲 Outliers, and the ones that move the line

The Gesell data: one child takes r² from 41% to 11%. Not resistant is not an abstraction.

Slides 41 and 42 — Gesell Adaptive Score against age at first word, 21 children. Solid line: all the data, r² = 41%. Dashed line: child 18 removed, r² = 11%. These are the classic textbook data behind the slide, and they reproduce the lecturer's two r² values exactly.

  • 🚨
    An outlier is an observation that lies outside the overall pattern of the other observations.
  • ⬆️
    Outliers in the y direction have large residuals — they sit far above or below the line. Child 19 is one.
  • ↔️
    Outliers in the x direction are often influential: removing them would markedly change the equation of the line. Child 18, at 42 months, is one.
  • 🪝
    Influence needs leverage. A point far out in x sits at the end of a long lever — so if its y is also off the trend of the rest, the line swings towards it. Far out in x and on the trend means leverage without influence.
  • 🕵️
    The dangerous case is the quiet one. A strongly influential point can have a small residual — because it has already dragged the line over to meet it.

★ Key insight

Never delete an influential point because it is inconvenient. The defensible move is to report both fits — "with child 18, r² = 41%; without him, 11%" — because the honest finding here is that the conclusion depends on one child.

🎬 Outliers, leverage and influence

🧩 Comprehension check

Is every outlier influential?
No. Child 19 is a clear outlier in y — a big residual — yet removing him leaves the slope almost unchanged (−1.13 to −1.19) and actually improves the fit. Influence requires leverage, which comes from being extreme in x.
Why is a small residual not proof that a point is harmless?
Because the line is fitted including that point. A high-leverage case pulls the line to itself, so its own residual ends up small by construction. The test is not the residual but the refit: take the point out and see whether the equation moves.
Would dropping child 18 be legitimate if his age at first word had been recorded in error?
Yes — a documented data error is a reason to correct or remove a value, and you say so in the write-up. What is not legitimate is removing him because the result is nicer without him. The rule is that the reason must exist independently of the effect on your conclusion.

Lesson 13 of 14 · r² · residual plots

🟢 How much did the line explain — and was it the right shape?

Two diagnostics that answer completely different questions. Students confuse them constantly; the exam does not.

Slide 43. The square of the correlation, r², is the fraction of the variation in the values of y that is explained by the least-squares regression of y on x. It is called the coefficient of determination.

  • 🟢
    r² is a fraction of variation, between 0 and 1, usually quoted as a percentage.
  • 🔢
    It is literally r squared, so r = 0.30 explains 9% — and the sign disappears, because a negative relationship explains just as much as a positive one.
  • 📝
    A residual is observed y − predicted y — the vertical distance between a point and the least-squares line.
  • 📉
    A residual plot puts the residuals against the explanatory variable. Ideally there should be a "random" scatter around zero.
  • ⚠️
    Residual patterns suggest deviations from a linear relationship — a curve means your model has the wrong shape, however high r² looked.

Left: data that really is linear — the residuals scatter randomly around zero. Right: a curved relationship fitted with a straight line — the residual plot bends, and that bend is the diagnosis. Both panels are illustrative.

📊 Two questions, two tools

ToolAnswers the questionDoes not answer
r²How much of the variation in y does this line account for?Whether a straight line was the right model at all — a curve can still produce a respectable r².
Residual plotIs a straight line the right shape, and is the spread constant?How strong the relationship is. A perfect residual plot is equally consistent with r² = 5% and r² = 95%.
🪝 Memory hook: r² is the score; the residual plot is the referee. A high score with the referee waving a flag is not a result.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "r² tells me what share of the differences in the outcome my predictor accounts for — 40% means 60% is still down to something else. Then I look at the leftovers: if they're random noise, a line was fine; if they make a pattern, I fitted the wrong shape."
★ Key insight

"Explained" in the definition of r² means accounted for by this line, not caused by x. The entire statistical content of the word is arithmetic about variation — the causal reading is smuggled in by the English, and Lesson 14 takes it back out.

🎬 r² and the coefficient of determination 🎬 Reading residual plots

🧩 Comprehension check

A study reports r = −0.5. What percent of the variation is explained, and by what?
25%, by the least-squares regression of y on x. The minus sign vanishes in the squaring, so a correlation of −0.5 and one of +0.5 explain exactly the same amount — they just point in opposite directions.
Your residual plot shows a funnel that widens as x increases. Which assumption is that?
Equal variance — the third assumption in Lesson 11. The line may still be the right shape, but the spread of y around it grows with x, so predictions are far less precise at high x than the model admits.
Why is a residual plot easier to read than the original scatterplot?
Because it removes the trend. Once the straight-line part is subtracted, the vertical scale can expand to show what is left, and a gentle curvature that was invisible under a steep slope becomes obvious around the zero line.

Lesson 14 of 14 · Extrapolation · lurking variables · causation

🚧 The four ways a good line still lies to you

Sarah at thirty, and the three diagrams that end the lecture.

Slides 49–52. Sarah's height against her age in months, with the fitted line ŷ = 71.95 + 0.383x. Inside the observed range (roughly 36–60 months) the line is excellent; the two predictions marked are 88 cm at 42 months and 209.8 cm at 360 months. The individual dots are illustrative — the slide gives the line, not the raw values.

  • 🚧
    Extrapolation is predicting y for an x outside the range you observed. At 42 months the line gives 88 cm — sensible. At 360 months it gives 209.8 cm: Sarah is predicted to be 6'10½" at thirty.
  • 🧩
    Lurking variables have an important effect on the relationship but are not included in the study.
  • 🔗
    Association, however strong, does NOT imply causation. The lecture's closing line, and the exam's favourite.
  • 🪞
    Both correlation and regression describe linear relationships, and both are affected by outliers. Always plot the data before interpreting.
  • 🧭
    Use caution predicting y when x is outside the range of the observed x's — the model has no evidence at all out there.

📊 Slide 54 — the closing checklist

  • Both are affected by outliers.
  • Always plot the data before interpreting.
  • Beware of extrapolation — use caution predicting y when x is outside the range of the observed x's.

🪝 Memory hook: extrapolation is reading past the end of the map. The map is not wrong — it simply stops, and the line keeps drawing anyway.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "The line only knows the range of x it was built from. Outside that it's making things up. And even inside it, a relationship can exist because x causes y, because something else causes both, or because the two are tangled together — and the data look identical in all three cases."
★ Key insight

Common response and confounding are not the same objection. In common response there is no arrow between x and y at all — z drives both. In confounding x may genuinely affect y, but z's effect runs alongside it and the two cannot be separated with these data.

🎬 Correlation is not causation 🎬 The danger of extrapolation 🎬 Confounding vs common response

🧩 Comprehension check

Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together across the year. Which diagram, and what is z?
Common response, with z = hot weather. Heat independently drives both ice-cream sales and swimming (hence drownings); there is no arrow from ice cream to drowning at all. The correlation is perfectly real — the causal reading is not.
Sarah's line fits her childhood data beautifully. Why is 209.8 cm not a criticism of the line?
Because the line was never told that growth stops. Within 36–60 months the straight-line model is a good description of her data; the error is ours, in asking it about an x value 300 months beyond anything it has ever seen. Extrapolation is a misuse of a good model, not a bad model.
In your own field: a company finds that employees who attend the leadership programme are promoted more often. Which diagram, and what would you do next?
Confounding is the honest first guess — the people selected for the programme were already rated as higher potential, so ambition and manager sponsorship (z) affect both attendance and promotion, tangled up with any real effect of the programme. The next step is not a bigger correlation but a design that separates them: compare against similarly rated employees who were not selected, or randomise who goes first.

🧵 Where this leaves you

★ The thesis of the whole lecture

A relationship between two quantitative variables is described by one picture and two numbers — and every warning in this chapter exists because the numbers cannot see the picture. r measures straight-line closeness only, so it under-reports a curve and over-reports a cloud with one stray point in it. The least-squares line fits itself to whatever you hand it, leans towards whichever point sits furthest out in x, and will predict a thirty-year-old at 6'10" without blinking. r² says how much of y the line accounts for; the residual plot says whether a line was ever the right shape; and none of it says that x caused y, because causation, common response and confounding all draw the same scatterplot. So the discipline is a sequence, not a formula: plot first, describe in words, then compute — and only then, very carefully, explain.
